In recent years, short-form video (SFV) platforms, most prominently TikTok, YouTube Shorts, and Instagram Reels, have emerged as dominant mediums for digital consumption and user engagement \cite{faverio_2025_teens}. Their scale is considerable: YouTube reported in 2025 that YouTube Shorts alone averages over 200 billion daily views, nearly tripling from 70 billion the previous year \cite{cobb_2025_youtube}. This trajectory reflects a structural shift in how audiences discover and consume media, away from long-form, search-driven viewing and toward algorithmically curated short-form feeds \cite{gottfried_2025_americans, lafayette_2025_the}.

Given these trends, SFV platforms are no longer just spaces for entertainment; they have become the primary stage for the global ``creator economy,'' valued at approximately \$254.4 billion in 2025 \cite{zoting_2026_creator}. For a growing population of creators, SFVs constitute not merely a medium for personal expression and branding but also a primary source of income \cite{violot2024shorts, varanasi_2025_tiktok}. However, the fast-paced and highly competitive nature of these platforms means that success is largely driven by fleeting user attention. On these platforms, viewers consume content through a rapid, binary ``swiping,'' pattern, either abandoning a video within its opening seconds or committing substantially more watch time to it, yielding a bimodal distribution of engagement \cite{li2024delving, sun2025engagement}; industry eye-tracking corroborates the decisiveness of these opening seconds, with 71\% of TikTok users watching at least the first three seconds of a video \cite{eyesee_2023_tap}. In this environment, early viewer-retention behavior functions not merely as an indicator of engagement but as a causal input to algorithmic distribution, determining whether content gains further visibility.

To sustain user interest, SFV platforms rely on algorithm-driven content distribution systems that expose videos to audiences based on early engagement patterns \cite{covington2016deep}. For content creators, this means that early engagement can strongly influence visibility and growth, while weak initial responses may limit reach regardless of content quality. Because early engagement thus governs downstream reach, forecasting it at the pre-upload stage becomes decision-critical for creators. A central challenge in this context is the cold-start scenario, where newly uploaded videos have little to no prior interaction data \cite{li2024delving}. In its classical formulation, cold-start denotes a recommender system's inability to route a new item lacking interaction history \cite{lika2014facing}; this study instead addresses its creator-side manifestation, in which the creator, not the platform, lacks the data needed to anticipate a video's performance at the moment of upload. Without early signals such as views or watch time, estimating a video's potential performance is difficult, rendering pre-publication decision-making uncertain. For the purposes of this study, engagement is operationalized as early viewer-retention behavior, specifically the Engagement Continuation Rate (ECR) and Normalized Average Watch Percentage (NAWP), rather than as aggregate social interaction.

To address this challenge, prior research has explored various computational approaches for predicting engagement under limited data conditions. Early studies relied on collaborative filtering and popularity-based indicators, which were limited by their dependence on historical interaction data \cite{he2017neural, zhou2010impact}. More recent work has shifted toward multimodal learning approaches, which analyze features directly extracted from videos, including visual, audio, and textual elements \cite{li2024delving, wu2018beyond}. The availability of large-scale datasets, such as SnapUGC and YouTube engagement datasets, has enabled the development of models that examine thumbnails, motion patterns, captions, and sound cues to estimate engagement even before significant user interaction occurs \cite{li2024delving, wu2018beyond}. Researchers have also proposed alternative engagement metrics, such as NAWP and ECR, to provide more nuanced measures beyond basic popularity indicators \cite{li2024delving}.

While these advances have improved prediction accuracy, most existing studies remain platform-centered. These models primarily support large-scale content distribution or post-upload performance analysis, rather than directly assisting content creators. In practice, creators often rely on intuition, trends, or trial-and-error when deciding what content to publish. Where creator-facing prediction does exist, it takes the form of
commercial tools such as Dash Social's Vision AI,\footnote{\label{fn:dashsocial}% 
Dash Social. (2026). \textit{Predictive AI tools for social media content}. Retrieved July 6, 2026, from
\url{https://www.dashsocial.com/features/predictive-ai}} which scores a post's visual composition against a brand's own content history; FeedHive,\footnote{\label{fn:feedhive}%
FeedHive. (2026). \textit{Social media automation}. Retrieved July 6, 2026, from \url{https://www.feedhive.com/}} which scores a draft against the creator's own prior posting
performance; and Predis.ai,\footnote{\label{fn:predisai}%
Predis.ai. (2026). \textit{AI social media post generator}. Retrieved July 6, 2026, from \url{https://predis.ai/}} which appends a performance estimate to its AI-generated content. These systems, however, remain undocumented outside their own marketing materials: none publishes its underlying feature schema, model architecture, or validation procedure, and none is validated against any publicly defined, behaviorally grounded retention metric, leaving their predictive validity unauditable. Consequently, no openly documented, creator-facing method exists for estimating potential engagement before uploading, leaving a significant gap in transparent pre-publication decision support.

Furthermore, engagement is influenced not only by video content but also by creator-related and contextual factors. Prior work highlights the role of creator characteristics, such as follower count, account age, and audience familiarity, in shaping engagement outcomes \cite{peng2024untangling, sadeque2019predicting, zannettou2024analyzing}. Social influence and creator-audience relationships affect how users respond to new uploads. In addition, contextual elements, including posting time and audience activity patterns, can influence engagement likelihood, as videos released during peak activity periods tend to receive higher reactions \cite{kanuri2018scheduling, manic2024short, spasojevic2015post}. Despite this evidence, creator and contextual factors are not consistently integrated into engagement prediction models, particularly in early-stage scenarios where interaction data is limited.

Given these gaps, there is a clear need for an engagement prediction approach that supports content creators before publication—one that unifies the multimodal, creator-related, and contextual signals that prior work has established individually but rarely combined. As the proprietary tools discussed above demonstrate, such prediction is already feasible to some extent; what remains absent is a transparent, reproducible, and empirically decompositional account of it based on established engagement metrics and concrete factors, leaving creators without an open understanding of how their videos might perform during the critical cold-start stage where early engagement determines visibility and reach. This study addresses that absence by developing a creator-oriented model that integrates upload-time creator and contextual metadata—signals available to the creator yet omitted by existing content-only cold-start models—into a frozen Large Multimodal Model ensemble, formulated as a regression task evaluated for both accuracy and ranking consistency. Specifically, it seeks to answer the question: \textbf{How does integrating creator-related and contextual features alongside multimodal content signals improve pre-publication engagement prediction accuracy for short-form videos?}

In alignment with the general objective, which is to investigate how integrated multimodal, creator-related, and contextual signals can improve engagement forecasting, the scope of this research is bounded by the selection of these three feature layers. The study will utilize a multimodal dataset of TikTok SFVs ranging from 5 to 60 seconds, incorporating raw MP4 files, from which the model internally derives visual and acoustic representations (content layer); follower counts and account age (creator layer); and specific posting hours and days (contextual layer). These variables constitute the initial feature set; while they provide the primary signals for this study, the methodology allows for the inclusion of additional features in future iterations to further refine the model's predictive resolution.

To ensure data consistency, the study is restricted to TikTok SFV content produced by Filipino micro-influencers recruited through a standardized data-donation process. In this study, a micro-influencer is operationally defined as an individual content creator managing an account with 10,000 to 100,000 followers whose primary target audience resides within the Philippines \cite{anymindgroup_2026_state}. Participation is strictly voluntary and creator-initiated: participants securely contribute their platform-verified analytics exports alongside corresponding video references directly to the research team \cite{zannettou2024analyzing, garimella_2025_data, li2024delving}. These donated analytics include the platform-reported ECR and NAWP values that serve as the study's ground-truth labels; because these values are computed by TikTok after a video has already been distributed, they represent engagement as realized under the platform's recommendation system rather than a video's intrinsic quality, and the study accordingly restricts its dataset to videos that have reached a stable measurement window of at least 1 month to limit label noise from still-maturing metrics. The study excludes nano- and macro-influencers, celebrity accounts, automated corporate profiles, and any data streams lacking explicit and audited owner consent. 

Consequently, a primary limitation of this approach is the potential for voluntary response bias. In addition, the voluntary nature of data donation introduces the possibility of self-selection bias, wherein participating creators may systematically differ from non-participants, such as by having greater confidence in their content performance or stronger interest in analytics. As a result, the donated dataset may not fully represent the broader population of TikTok creators and may yield a smaller, more specialized sample than large-scale automated scraping would provide, which may affect the broader representativeness of the findings. More consequentially, because the dataset is drawn exclusively from Filipino micro-influencers within a narrow follower band, the findings may not generalize to other creator tiers, geographic markets, or content verticals, including elsewhere on TikTok itself. Captions in this population are moreover frequently written in Tagalog or code-switched Taglish, for which the predominantly English- and Chinese-pretrained LMM backbones offer unvalidated comprehension, rendering the caption feature a recognized source of measurement uncertainty. Finally, the statistical power of the study's significance tests remains contingent on the final volume of donated videos, which will be reported alongside the data partition.

Regarding the predictive model, the scope centers on an ensemble architecture of Large Multimodal Models (LMMs) — specifically VideoLLaMA2 and Qwen2.5-VL — designed to address the cold-start problem by performing numerical engagement regression using only signals available at the exact moment of upload. Following \cite{sun2025engagement}, the video is represented by sampled keyframes and emotional audio cues rather than by the full stream. Owing to computational resource constraints, the study is limited to frozen LMM backbones used as zero-shot predictors of ECR and NAWP; the frozen backbones therefore contain no trainable parameters, and where the study introduces learnable parameters, these are confined to a lightweight auxiliary model over creator and contextual metadata rather than the backbones themselves. Full end-to-end fine-tuning of the backbones is out of scope \cite{li2025vquala}. Furthermore, because the framework is optimized for the swiping mechanics and algorithmic patterns of the TikTok ecosystem, the generalizability of the model to other short-form platforms remains a methodological boundary.

For evaluation, the study adopts ECR and NAWP as its exclusive target variables. ECR measures the probability of retaining a viewer past the initial five-second ``hook,'' while NAWP provides a duration-normalized measure of total viewer retention. This boundary deliberately excludes traditional social metrics such as raw view counts, like rates, and shares, as these are prone to duration bias and fail to capture genuine user preference under the bimodal distribution characteristic of SFV consumption \cite{sun2025engagement}. The study assesses predictive accuracy through RMSE, SRCC, and PLCC, which jointly bound the evaluation to error magnitude and to the rank and linear agreement between predicted and observed scores; it does not assess model calibration or decision-threshold utility for an individual creator. To support a generalizable claim, the finalized MicroTok-PH dataset will be partitioned using a creator-level, creator-disjoint, stratified strategy, with 70\% of creators allocated to the training set, 15\% to the validation set, and 15\% to the testing set. All videos from a given creator will be assigned exclusively to one partition to prevent information leakage arising from creator-specific characteristics such as editing style, audience demographics, and posting behavior. Creators will first be stratified by follower-count ranges, after which creator-disjoint sampling will be performed within each stratum to preserve the distribution of the target micro-influencer population across all partitions. The 70/15/15 partition provides sufficient data for model training while reserving independent subsets for hyperparameter tuning and unbiased performance evaluation, making it a widely adopted practice in supervised machine learning.

A resulting limitation of this focus is that the research optimizes for immediate, algorithmically-mediated retention rather than long-term virality or total social-interaction volume \cite{li2024delving}. Finally, the study benchmarks the proposed model against LMM-EVQA \cite{sun2025engagement} and MMF-QE \cite{guan2025mmf} on this same dataset; because these two benchmarks were originally developed on different label distributions, the comparison establishes relative performance within this study's context rather than a universal ranking of the models  \cite{li2025vquala}.

The rapid growth of SFV platforms has created a landscape where digital visibility is increasingly shaped by algorithm-driven engagement dynamics. In this context, the ability to anticipate audience response before publishing content has become a critical challenge for creators operating in highly competitive and fast-moving environments. By developing a predictive framework that integrates multimodal content signals with creator-related and contextual factors, this study will contribute to a more proactive and creator-centered understanding of engagement modeling.

Significantly, this study will offer practical implications for content creators themselves. By exploring how different feature categories contribute to engagement outcomes, the study will yield two practical outputs: quantitative pre-publication estimates of a video's expected ECR and NAWP, and category-level evidence, via the ablation study, of how much creator and contextual factors add beyond content alone. These estimates are diagnostic in a way vanity metrics such as likes, shares, or raw views are not, since those figures are confounded by algorithmic redistribution and existing follower reach rather than the content itself: ECR isolates whether a video's opening hook is strong enough to act on before publishing, while NAWP isolates whether its pacing sustains attention across its full length, regardless of how many views either video eventually receives. Such insights may help creators move beyond intuition-based strategies acnd toward more data-informed creative workflows, without requiring deep technical expertise.

From a computer science perspective, this research will extend existing work in multimodal learning and engagement prediction by shifting the focus from platform optimization toward pre-publication decision support. While prior studies have primarily explored engagement modeling for large-scale distribution systems, this work will investigate how predictive models can be adapted for early-stage estimation. The integration of content, creator, and contextual features offers a more holistic modeling approach that may inform future developments in multimodal machine learning, human-centered AI, and applied predictive analytics. In contrast to commercial tools such as
Dash Social\footref{fn:dashsocial},
FeedHive\footref{fn:feedhive},
and Predis.ai\footref{fn:predisai}, whose scoring remains proprietary, this study will specify its features, architecture, and evaluation openly, contributing to human-centered AI—understood here as systems that augment rather than automate human judgment—by informing rather than replacing the creator's decision.

Beyond technical contributions, this study will carry economic relevance within the growing creator economy. As short-form content increasingly functions as a source of livelihood, the ability to estimate engagement before posting may help creators make more informed production decisions, reduce trial-and-error costs, and allocate time and creative resources more strategically. Predictive insights may also support emerging creators who lack access to large audiences or analytics tools, potentially lowering barriers to entry and promoting more sustainable participation in digital content markets.

At a societal level, enabling creators to better understand engagement dynamics may contribute to a more diverse and balanced content ecosystem. When creators can evaluate potential performance earlier, they may be more willing to experiment with niche topics, educational content, or underrepresented narratives that might otherwise be overshadowed by purely trend-driven production. In this sense, an open, creator-oriented predictive approach may indirectly support content diversity, digital inclusion, and more meaningful audience–creator interactions.

Finally, this work will establish a foundation for future research at the intersection of engagement prediction and creator-centered technologies. By demonstrating the feasibility of pre-upload engagement estimation, the study will open directions for further exploration in areas such as creator-assistive tools, intelligent content feedback systems, and human--AI collaboration in digital media production. In doing so, it reframes engagement prediction not only as a platform-level optimization problem but as an opportunity to design technologies that directly empower creators.
